The look of the DAR film

What I took from your three TikTok references, what Lebanese construction looks like on camera, and real photos of it. The Higgsfield keyframes will be built from this brief.

1. Your TikTok references

I read each one frame by frame.

“Built to last.” hero

@elyptwebdesign · 21 s · construction hero section, day 5/30

One fixed, low ground-level camera. As you scroll the same plot changes: empty land at sunset → excavator digging → timber frame → finished modern villa at golden hour. Big white headline on the left.

What I like

  • Same plot, same angle, time moves forward. Instantly readable “from land to building” story, which is exactly DAR.
  • Warm golden-hour / dusk grade. Feels premium, not like a stock photo.
  • Very little UI. The footage does the selling.

What I don't like

  • Timber-frame American/Australian house. Nobody builds like that in Lebanon.
  • The camera never moves. No drone, no flying in, no detail of the work.
  • It's only a hero section. It stops before showing how the work is done.

“Building Los Angeles since 2009”

@optimawebai · 20 s · one-page construction company site

Hero is a real concrete-frame site (columns with rebar sticking out). On scroll the camera pushes through a doorway into a finished interior. Then dark sections, a yellow accent, condensed uppercase headings (“Complete construction solutions”, “Construction you can rely on”), service cards and a project grid.

What I like

  • The camera enters the building. That is your “drone goes into the center” idea.
  • Concrete columns + starter rebar: the closest of the three to how Lebanon builds.
  • Dark background with one accent colour, which maps directly onto DAR black + orange.
  • Strong, short headlines. Project gallery as proof.

What I don't like

  • From site to interior is a jump/morph, not one continuous flight, so you lose the “we did all of this” feeling.
  • Everything below the hero is a generic template (cards, grids, yellow-and-black safety cliché).

“A plan… And then, a skyline.”

@webloved · 23 s · before/after architecture scroll (GSAP)

Opens on blue architectural line drawings that become a real house in a green field. Then a split screen: dark blueprint on one side (“A plan”), finished render on the other, with the two halves scrolling slightly out of sync. Ends on “And then, a skyline.” in an elegant italic over a city.

What I like

  • Blueprint → real building = DAR's “from the first drawing to the keys”.
  • The split “plan vs result” is the perfect picture of Results First. Payment Follows. What was promised next to what was delivered.
  • Calm, editorial copy that tells a story in very few words.

What I don't like

  • European villa and a generic skyline. No Lebanese identity.
  • It's a slideshow of stills, not a continuous drone flight.
  • Light, pale theme. Too soft for DAR's bold brand.

“Architecture made here” → “To the last light”

@elyptwebdesign · 16 s · architecture studio hero, day 15/30

Starts as a flat black-and-white floor plan seen from straight above, with a serif headline. On scroll the plan grows into a furnished 3D top-down cutaway (rooms, pool, cars in the garage). Then the camera tilts down to eye level and it becomes the real villa at dusk, every light on: “To the last light”.

What I like

  • Plan → 3D cutaway → real building from one camera that tilts. That is the best opening I've seen for DAR.
  • The top-down cutaway is the clearest way to show inside a building: shop by shop, floor by floor, even the pipes in the floor before the tiles go on.
  • The payoff is dusk with the lights on, the same ending as our handover.
  • “To the last light” echoes DAR's promise “to the last detail”.

What I don't like

  • Luxury villa with a pool. Not a Lebanese mini mall.
  • Too perfect: the cutaway has that plastic AI-render look and fake furniture.
  • No construction at all. It jumps from drawing to finished.
  • Thin serif type is elegant but too soft for DAR's bold brand.

What I'll take into DAR's film: open on the mini mall's floor plan from straight above that grows into a 3D cutaway, then the drone tilts to the real empty plot like video 4. Then one continuous drone flight (not cuts) over one plot that changes stage by stage like video 1. The camera flies into the building to show the work like video 2, and the top-down cutaway comes back to show the pipes in the floor and each shop, office and home. The “what we promised / what we delivered” split carries the payment message like video 3, and it ends at dusk with every light on. Everything is Lebanese, everything is dark + DAR orange, and the headlines are short.

2. What Lebanese construction looks like

The details that make a viewer in Lebanon think “that's here”. Every keyframe prompt will include these.

Concrete frame + hollow-block wallsReinforced concrete columns and slabs, walls filled in with grey hollow concrete blocks (“bloc”), then rendered and painted. No timber framing.
Rebar left on the roofStarter bars sticking up from the top slab “for the next floor” — the most Lebanese sight on any rooftop.
Plywood formwork and steel propsSlabs poured on plywood and rows of adjustable steel props (shores); voided slabs with plastic/polystyrene void formers are common in Beirut.
Pour dayConcrete mixer trucks and a pump boom, workers spreading and vibrating the slab, often at dawn to beat the heat.
Pipes and conduits in the floorPPR water pipes (green/white), PVC drains and corrugated electrical conduits laid on the bare slab, then buried under sand fill and screed, then tiles or marble on top.
Natural stone facadesCream sandstone/limestone cladding with hand-dressed finishes on villas and better buildings; triple-arch windows as the heritage signature.
Deep balconiesWide balconies with plants, aluminium railings or glass, and striped fabric awnings.
The rooftop clutterWater tanks, solar water heaters, solar panels, satellite dishes. Every Lebanese roof has them.
Generator cablesBundles of “ishtirak” generator wires along the street. One or two strands = real; a tangle = too much for a premium brand.
Ground-floor shopsRows of shops with metal roll-up shutters and illuminated signs under the first floor, often bilingual Arabic/English.
The settingDense Beirut blocks with the sea on one side and Mount Lebanon behind; pine and olive trees; terraced hillsides outside the city.
The lightHard Mediterranean sun, pale dust in the air, warm golden hour and a pink-orange dusk over the sea.
Finishing tradesGypsum-board false ceilings with spotlights, porcelain and marble floors, epoxy in shops and parking, interlocking pavers outside.
People and kitWorkers in hard hats and DAR orange vests, a site engineer with drawings, a tower crane over the neighbourhood, a pickup and a mixer at the gate.

4. What we'll avoid

  • Timber-framed or American suburban houses
  • Snow, lawns, brick, or anything that looks like Europe or the US
  • Glass-tower Dubai look for a mini mall
  • Messy, unsafe sites (no helmets, collapsing scaffolds)
  • Pipes and cables fixed on walls or ceilings
  • War damage or crisis imagery. DAR is about trust and security
  • Fake brand names or real company logos on signs
  • Hard cuts between shots. It should feel like one flight
